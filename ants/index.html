Ant Ranchers

Ants really do farm aphids: they carry them off a plant, keep them underground, stroke their backsides with their antennae to make them give up honeydew, and tear apart any ladybird that comes for the herd. Every beat in this film is a real behaviour.

Is any of this actually true?

The milking is real

Ants stroke and drum an aphid's abdomen with their antennae, and the aphid responds by releasing a droplet of honeydew, which the ant drinks on the spot. Tended aphids hold the droplet for the ant instead of flicking it away, and they release it faster and more often than untended ones.

Common mix-up

It comes out the back

Honeydew is surplus plant sap, excreted from the anus. The two horns on an aphid's rear — the cornicles — are not milk taps: they squirt a waxy alarm pheromone when the aphid is attacked. Nearly every diagram that labels them as spouts has it wrong.

The kidnapping is husbandry

Ants pick aphids up in their mandibles and carry them to fresher stems, move them out of bad weather, and take aphid eggs down into the nest to overwinter, putting them back out on plants in spring. Some ants also farm root aphids entirely underground.

So is the violence

Ants guarding a herd attack ladybirds, lacewings and parasitic wasps: they bite at the legs, spray formic acid, and will dismember ladybird larvae and eat the eggs. Aphid colonies with ants on them survive predators far better than colonies without.

Cast: Lasius niger, the black garden ant · Macrosiphum rosae, the rose aphid · Coccinella septempunctata, the seven-spot ladybird.
